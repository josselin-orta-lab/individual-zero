## Read-only follow-up question

Does lower replay work translate into an overall improvement in both *actual energy and vigilance* per assigned life, or can observed survival changes, shorter lives and ecology-specific tradeoffs account for misleading aggregate energy signals? Which additional matched comparator would be required to distinguish GATE policy effects from a paid but random replay schedule?

This analysis was determined **after** the B1 survival data were observed. It is a finite descriptive decomposition, not a prespecified test or causal mediation estimate. The paired group labels below are themselves defined by outcomes, so any association conditional on them is subject to severe selection/collider bias.

The numbers above use the original 192 complete fixed-horizon assignments (96 per arm), never only the survivors or only observed sleep steps. Sleep/replay spending is a real measured physical exposure; the two cognitive currencies have distinct real costs. A higher GATE vigilance bill despite fewer replays disproves the simple claim that fewer replays guarantees lower **total** cognition cost in both currencies.

The GATE-only-survivor group lives much longer and therefore often pays **more aggregate** cognition, while the FIXED-only-survivor group often ends prematurely under GATE and pays much less. These opposing duration effects make total-cognition savings particularly sensitive to who survives. The stable/depletion/pulse effects likewise differ; in pulse, GATE spends more **both** energy and vigilance per assigned life while having higher observed survival. Do not assign GATE a universal benefit or infer that cognitive replay saving *caused* survival improvement.

At the terminal boundary, 39 GATE lives attained 400 steps but only 38 were horizon survivors: one died on step 400 and must not be recoded as censored. The source `terminal` label, not `steps_lived >= 400`, defines survival.

## Scientific consequence and smallest next discriminating experiment
